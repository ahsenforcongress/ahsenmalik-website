Climate policy tends to get presented as a binary choice: either you believe
in aggressive government action to eliminate fossil fuels as fast as
possible, or you're a climate skeptic standing in the way of progress. That
binary doesn't reflect where most Americans, or most serious energy
experts, are.

## Why Fossil Fuels Aren't Going Away on Their Own Timeline

Here is a fact that should inform how we think about this problem: even
China, the country investing more aggressively in solar, wind, and battery
storage than any other nation on earth, still generates more than half of
its electricity from fossil fuels today. That tells us clean electricity
hasn't yet been built out at the scale a modern industrial economy
requires, anywhere in the world. It doesn't let the United States off the
hook. We built our economy first and emitted more, cumulatively and per
person, than any country on earth to get here, and that history means we
have a responsibility to lead on bringing the next generation of clean
technology to scale, not an excuse to wait for someone else to solve it
first.

It's worth being precise about what "not ready" means here, because the
honest answer is different depending on the sector. For power generation,
solar, wind, batteries, and nuclear are all proven technologies. What's
lagging for renewables is deployment pace, particularly long-duration
battery storage: PJM, our own regional grid operator, has barely begun to
build storage capacity compared to grids like Texas and California, and
storage is one of the most important levers, alongside transmission
buildout, for turning intermittent generation into dependable capacity.
What's lagging for nuclear in the U.S. specifically is a construction and
permitting pipeline that can deliver new plants on time and on budget,
something Georgia's Vogtle project illustrated at real cost, running
roughly double its original budget over about 15 years. For a smaller but
stubborn slice of the economy — a fifth to a third of global emissions
depending on how broadly hard-to-abate sectors are defined, from steel,
cement, and chemicals to aviation and shipping — the challenge is
different and harder: their emissions come from the chemical processes
and material inputs themselves, not just the fuel used to power them, and
while pilot-scale projects are advancing, genuinely low-carbon
alternatives at scale remain a work in progress. A serious energy plan
must be honest about that difference: build out mature clean power as
fast as we can and be upfront that the hardest parts of industrial
decarbonization don't yet have a proven, at-scale answer.

Battery chemistry, grid storage, nuclear power, and other technologies are
advancing quickly, and I support pushing that progress as hard as we can
because a serious energy policy must be built for the world we live in
today while actively building the one we need for tomorrow.

## Why This Has to Be Bipartisan

None of this is simple, and none of the tradeoffs are easy. Moving too
aggressively away from fossil fuels before reliable alternatives exist at
scale risks energy price spikes, grid instability, and real economic pain
for working families. Moving too slowly risks the consequences of unchecked
climate change continuing to compound. Getting that balance right requires
genuinely careful, technical, good-faith judgment, not a bill written by
one party to satisfy its own base, then reversed by the other party as soon
as power changes hands. A bipartisan bill, built by members of both parties
who have to actually defend it to their own constituents, is far more
likely to land on tradeoffs that can survive a change in political power,
and far more likely to get the engineering judgment right rather than the
ideological talking point right.

## The Permitting Problem: Where Bipartisanship Is Already Happening

Here's something most voters don't know: the federal approval process for
any major energy project — a new transmission line, a solar farm, a
pipeline, a nuclear plant, a geothermal well — is governed by a 1970 law
called the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA). Today, it routinely
takes federal agencies four and a half years on average just to complete
the environmental review for a major energy project, and legal challenges
can add years more on top of that. This slows down everything: clean energy
projects and fossil fuel projects alike. It is not a partisan problem. It
is an infrastructure problem, and it is hindering our ability to build
anything quickly in this country, at exactly the moment electricity demand
is surging.

The good news is that permitting reform is one of the few issues in
Washington right now where genuine bipartisan work is already happening,
and it deserves more attention than it's getting. The House passed the
SPEED Act in December 2025, and several similar bipartisan bills are moving
alongside it in both chambers. None of these have been signed into law yet,
and as written, they're built to be strictly technology-neutral: treating a
pipeline and a solar farm the same. I don't think that's the right goal,
and I'm not shy about saying reform should deliberately favor cleaner
energy sources rather than pretend every technology is starting from the
same place. Fossil fuel infrastructure has decades of built-out pipelines,
existing grid connections, and established permitting relationships behind
it. Clean energy projects are the newer entrants, and they
disproportionately depend on new transmission that doesn't exist yet.
Treating an incumbent with a decades-long head start the same as a newer
competitor still building its first connections isn't neutral in practice,
it just locks in the advantage the incumbent already has.

To be clear about what I'm proposing and what I'm not: I'm not proposing
to rewrite NEPA's substantive standards along partisan lines. I'm
proposing a separate, dedicated federal fast lane and funding stream,
sitting alongside the existing NEPA process, that specifically targets the
biggest bottleneck holding back new clean energy: transmission. Today, the
real chokepoint usually isn't the federal environmental review at all,
it's slow-moving state siting fights and utility interconnection queues
that can leave a fully built solar or wind project sitting idle for years,
unable to connect to the grid. That's exactly why federal backstop siting
authority matters: it gives the federal government a tool to step in and
get interstate transmission built when state and utility-level processes
stall, which is the specific, documented obstacle slowing clean energy
today. Fossil fuel projects would continue through the same review process
and timelines in place today, with no rollback of environmental standards
and no new fast lane either. The point of permitting reform should be to
help build the energy system we need for the future, not to hand fossil
fuel infrastructure an equal seat at the front of the line it's already in.

## What I Support

I support a bipartisan climate bill that:

- **Aggressively expands clean and renewable energy** — solar, wind,
  nuclear, geothermal, and next-generation battery storage — wherever it
  can be deployed at reasonable cost, without compromising grid
  reliability, and without compromising public safety and health.
  "Reasonable cost" means real, time-limited, capped incentives that get
  evaluated and phased down as technologies mature, not open-ended
  subsidies that lose cost discipline over time. Where a given technology
  can't be deployed at reasonable cost, that's not a reason to subsidize
  it indefinitely, it's a signal of a market failure that deserves its
  own honest, technical discussion of exactly how much we'd spend against
  exactly how much emissions reduction we'd actually get.
- **Acknowledges that fossil fuels will remain part of the energy mix for
  the foreseeable future** and takes a pragmatic, realistic approach to
  reducing their impact over time, rather than locking in a rigid timeline
  that ignores real-world engineering and economic constraints.
- **Invests in the mineral supply chains and domestic manufacturing
  capacity** that any serious clean energy transition will require. This
  is a real tradeoff worth being honest about: continuing to rely on oil
  and gas means continuing to accept higher domestic carbon emissions,
  while shifting toward clean technology means relying on battery
  minerals whose supply chains are today largely controlled by other
  countries, China in particular. I don't think either side of that
  tradeoff should be accepted blindly, which is exactly why building up
  our own domestic mineral supply chains and processing capacity matters:
  it's how we lower emissions without simply trading one form of
  dependence for another.
- **Is written and negotiated by members of both parties**, so that the
  tradeoffs it makes reflect a durable consensus rather than a temporary
  majority and so the resulting policy survives long enough for
  businesses and families to plan around it.
- **Includes real permitting reform**, building on the bipartisan momentum
  already underway in Congress, by funding federal agencies to clear
  their review backlogs and creating a dedicated federal backstop and
  fast lane for the transmission lines and clean energy projects stuck
  for years in state-level siting fights and utility interconnection
  queues, so approved projects can't be cancelled by political whiplash
  after developers have already invested.
- **Protects the communities that have carried the heaviest burden** from
  power plants, refineries, and industrial facilities, by preserving full
  environmental review and community input for new fossil fuel
  infrastructure and directing dedicated funding toward air and water
  quality improvements in the frontline neighborhoods most affected.
- **Backs all workers affected** as the energy mix changes, not just
  those in extraction, with real transition support: job retraining, wage
  replacement, and pension protection for workers in power generation,
  transportation, manufacturing, and supply chains, so the cost of that
  change doesn't fall on the workers and families who had no say in it.
- **Also addresses issues beyond energy sources** including urban sprawl
  and agricultural and food systems.

Virginians in our district don't need another climate bill that becomes a
political football. They need an honest plan that takes the problem
seriously, takes the engineering constraints seriously, and is built to
last longer than the next election.
