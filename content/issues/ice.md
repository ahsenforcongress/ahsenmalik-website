---
title: "ICE: To Abolish or Not, That Is the Question"
description: "Ahsen Malik's position on ICE, immigration enforcement, and undocumented immigrants."
---

I don't support a slogan, and I don't support abusive, unaccountable
enforcement tactics either. I support a secure border, an immigration
enforcement system that follows the law and answers to the public, and a
realistic, honest plan for the millions of undocumented people already
living in this country. That plan is not open borders, and it is not mass
deportation. It's permanent legal residency, without a path to citizenship.

Most people don't realize immigration enforcement predates ICE. Before
2003, these functions sat inside the INS. ICE, CBP, and USCIS were only
split out after 9/11 as part of the DHS reorganization. Most serious
"abolish ICE" proposals don't call for ending enforcement, including
enforcement against visa overstays. They call for reorganizing how
enforcement is carried out and by whom. Others go further, arguing for a
compliance-based model over detention and mass removal. And a growing
number of voices on the center-left explicitly reject the "abolish"
framing altogether, while still demanding an end to masked, unidentified
agents, excessive force, and a lack of transparency.

I don't think the label matters nearly as much as the substance. Whatever
we call the agency, it needs clear rules of engagement, identifiable
officers, use-of-force accountability, and real oversight. That's not a
radical position. It's a baseline for any law enforcement agency operating
in American communities.

## The Heart of the Issue: Mass Deportation of the Undocumented

Once you set aside the debate over ICE's name and org chart, the actual
policy question is what to do about the roughly 10–15 million undocumented
immigrants already living in this country, most of whom crossed the border
years ago. Physically locating, detaining, and removing millions of people
is not a realistic plan. It seems that the goal of ICE thuggery is to
terrify the undocumented into self-deporting. It hasn't worked, and instead
resulted in a backlash, even among people who initially supported a
tougher approach in theory.

With illegal crossings down substantially from their recent peak, I
believe it's time to pair a secure border with an honest resolution for
people already here: permanent legal residency, with no path to
citizenship. This is not amnesty. It does not grant voting rights or a
route to naturalization. It does mean people who have been contributing to
their communities can stop living in fear, stop being exploited by
employers who use their status against them, and be accounted for instead
of remaining in the shadows. Ending that exploitation also helps raise
wages and working conditions for everyone, including American-born
workers.

I recognize this satisfies neither the far left nor the far right
entirely, and I expect to be pressed from both directions. To advocates
who want a citizenship path, I'd say: legal status without fear of removal
is a substantial, meaningful improvement over the status quo, and I
believe the overwhelming majority of undocumented immigrants will be
grateful and satisfied with it. To those who want mass removal, I'd ask:
what is your actual, physically achievable alternative? Do you want
President Trump to start executing undocumented immigrants in order to
terrify the rest of them into self-deporting? Even that probably won't
work, but it will for sure trigger a global backlash against us.

Separately, I believe our overall legal immigration quotas are too low,
and I support increasing them. But that must happen through legislation,
debated openly in Congress, with the American people persuaded on the
merits, not through executive workarounds. Sidestepping that debate is
part of what has fueled a broader backlash against legal immigration
itself, and I don't think that serves anyone.

None of this requires tolerating abusive tactics. Masked agents who won't
identify themselves, unmarked vehicles, and unaccountable uses of force
erode public trust and make communities less safe, not more. Enforcement
of the law and respect for basic constitutional norms are not in conflict.
I will support clear identification requirements, use-of-force standards,
and independent oversight for any agency carrying out interior immigration
enforcement.

## Why This Should Concern Every American, Not Just the Undocumented

Following passage of the 2026 budget reconciliation bill, ICE received
roughly $45 billion for new detention capacity, on top of billions more
for enforcement, making it the largest-funded federal law enforcement
agency in the country. Internal planning documents describe $38.3 billion
in warehouse conversions, including eight "mega-centers" holding up to
10,000 people each, plus sixteen regional processing sites. Detention
capacity has grown from under 40,000 people at the start of this
administration to roughly 70,000 today, with a stated goal of adding
another 100,000 beds by the end of November 2026.

Large-scale detention infrastructure, once built, doesn't disappear when
the original justification for it fades, and it doesn't limit itself to
the population it was built for. American history has examples of exactly
this: the same government machinery used to intern Japanese Americans
during World War II wasn't purpose-built for that; it was adapted from
existing authority and infrastructure. Domestic surveillance programs
built to target specific threats have, at various points in our history,
been redirected against anti-war protesters, civil rights leaders, and
political dissidents who posed no threat at all. That's not a partisan
claim about any single president. It's a pattern in how unchecked
government capacity tends to get used over time.

That's why I take seriously the concerns raised by people across the
political spectrum about where this kind of capacity could lead:
civil-liberties advocates on the left have warned about detention
infrastructure being turned against anti-war protesters or draft resisters
in a future conflict. This is not just theoretical — given the current
trend of events, there is a serious possibility President Trump will send
ground troops to invade Iran, and that likely would require reinstituting
the draft. Second Amendment advocates on the right have raised similar
warnings about emergency powers being used against lawful gun owners after
a crisis. A government with 170,000 detention beds has built a tool that
will outlast the political moment that created it.
